Lossless vs Lossy Compression

The single idea behind why some files are tiny and others are huge.

Almost every file format you use makes a choice between two kinds of compression: lossless and lossy. Understanding the difference helps you pick the right format every time — and explains why a photo can be 300 KB while the "same" image is 4 MB.

Lossless compression

Lossless compression makes a file smaller without throwing anything away. When you open the file, you get back exactly what went in — every pixel, every sample. It works by storing repetition more efficiently (for example, "500 white pixels" instead of listing each one). Examples: PNG images, FLAC audio, and ZIP archives. The trade-off is that the savings are modest, so files stay relatively large.

Lossy compression

Lossy compression makes files dramatically smaller by permanently discarding information humans are least likely to notice — subtle colour shifts, sounds masked by louder ones. The result is "good enough" and far smaller. Examples: JPG images, MP3 audio, and MP4 video. The catch: every time you re-save a lossy file, you lose a little more — quality only goes down, never back up.

Side-by-side

  • Lossless: perfect quality, larger files — PNG, FLAC, ZIP.
  • Lossy: tiny files, slight quality loss — JPG, MP3, MP4, WebP (lossy mode).

How to choose

Use lossless when quality is critical or you will keep editing: master audio, archival photos, logos, screenshots with text. Use lossy for the final, shareable version where small size wins: web photos, podcasts, videos. A common professional workflow is to edit in lossless and export to lossy only at the very end.

Why this explains "huge" files

A WAV (lossless, uncompressed) can be ten times bigger than an MP3 of the same recording; a PNG photo can dwarf the JPG version. Neither is "wrong" — they are simply optimised for different goals. See it in action in our WAV to MP3 and PNG vs JPG guides.

Frequently asked questions

Does converting lossy → lossless restore quality? No. Once detail is discarded it is gone; converting an MP3 to WAV just makes a big file of the already-reduced audio.

Is lossy bad? Not at all — it powers the modern web. It is only a problem when you re-compress the same file many times.
