How Image Compression Works

Why a photo can shrink from 8 MB to 800 KB and still look great.

Every digital photo is, at heart, a grid of coloured dots called pixels. A modern phone photo has tens of millions of them, and storing the exact colour of each one would be enormous. Image compression is the clever set of tricks that shrinks that data — sometimes by 90% — while keeping the picture looking the same to your eyes. Here is how it works, without the maths.

Step 1: spotting redundancy

Photos are full of repetition: a blue sky is thousands of nearly-identical pixels. Compression finds these patterns and stores them compactly — "this whole region is roughly this blue" — instead of describing each pixel separately. This part is lossless: nothing is thrown away, the data is just written more efficiently.

Step 2: dropping what you won't notice

To get really small, lossy formats like JPG and WebP go further. Human eyes are very sensitive to brightness but much less sensitive to subtle colour changes, so these formats keep detail where you notice it and simplify it where you don't. They work in small blocks of the image, smoothing fine variations that your eye would gloss over anyway. Turn the quality down too far and those blocks become visible as "artefacts."

Quality settings: the dial you control

The quality slider you see when exporting a JPG controls how aggressive that second step is. At 90–100%, the loss is invisible and the file is moderately small. At 60–80%, the file shrinks a lot with only minor loss — ideal for the web. Below that, you start to see blurring and blocky edges, especially around text.

Why format choice matters

Different formats compress differently. PNG is lossless and best for sharp graphics; JPG is lossy and great for photos; WebP can do both and is typically 25–35% smaller than either. Choosing the right one for the job is the easiest way to get a small file that still looks good — see our best image format for the web and lossless vs lossy guides.

Practical tips to shrink images well

  • Resize first: a 6000-pixel-wide photo displayed at 1200px wastes data — scale it down.
  • Pick the right format: JPG/WebP for photos, PNG for graphics and text.
  • Use 70–85% quality for web images; reserve 90%+ for printing.
  • Avoid re-saving lossy files repeatedly — each save loses a little more.

Frequently asked questions

Does compression always reduce quality? Lossless compression does not; lossy does, but at high settings the loss is invisible.

What's the easiest win? Resizing the image to the size you actually display it at — it often matters more than the format.
